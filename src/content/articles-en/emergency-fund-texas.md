An emergency fund is not a savings goal; it is insurance against having to borrow at the worst possible moment. The right size is determined by three things: your essential monthly costs, how stable your income is, and how large a deductible you would face in a real emergency.

## 1. Measure essentials, not total spending

Start from costs you cannot stop paying next month:

| Category | Include? | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Rent or mortgage | Yes | Include taxes and insurance if escrowed |
| Utilities and internet | Yes | Minimum viable amount |
| Groceries | Yes | A realistic baseline, not a cut-to-the-bone figure |
| Insurance premiums | Yes | Health, auto, home |
| Transport to work | Yes | Fuel, payments, minimal maintenance |
| Minimum debt payments | Yes | Contractual minimums only |
| Subscriptions and dining | No | These are the first to pause |

Add these up. That number, not your total spending, is what the fund must replace.

## 2. Pick a target by risk, not by a slogan

Three-month and six-month targets are starting points, not rules:

- **Stable salaried income, two earners**: three months of essentials is a reasonable floor.
- **Single earner or variable income**: six months is more appropriate.
- **Contract or commission work, or a sector with frequent layoffs**: consider nine months.
- **One income plus dependants and a mortgage**: bias upward.

## 3. Add the deductibles

Emergency funds also cover the bills insurance does not pay immediately. List your deductibles (health, home, auto) and add the largest one to the target. A fund that covers living costs but not the roof deductible is incomplete.

## 4. Where to keep the money

The fund needs to be reachable within one working day without market risk:

- High-yield savings account, separate from your daily account.
- Money market deposit account at an insured institution.
- A short-term instrument that matures within days, if you prefer slightly more yield.

Avoid keeping the fund in investments that can fall in value, and avoid keeping it in the account you spend from; convenience is what quietly spends it.

## 5. Build it in a fixed order

1. Open a separate account and name it for its purpose.
2. Automate a transfer on payday, even if small.
3. Send any windfall, bonus or refund straight to it.
4. Pause non-essential subscriptions until the first month is funded.
5. Stop adding only when you reach the target, then direct new savings elsewhere.

## 6. Rules for using it

- Define what counts as an emergency in writing before you need it.
- Replace what you use within a fixed period, or the fund becomes a spending account.
- Do not use it to cover a predictable expense; that is what planned savings are for.

## 7. Common mistakes

- Counting retirement accounts as an emergency fund.
- Building a fund while carrying high-interest card balances; usually the card goes first.
- Keeping the entire fund in cash at home.
- Setting a target so ambitious that you never finish and give up.
- Forgetting that a job loss also means insurance and transport costs continue.
